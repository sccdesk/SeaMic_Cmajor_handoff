2. Theoretical Framework

2.1 Loudness measurement: ITU-R BS.1770 and EBU R128

The modern standard for perceived loudness is ITU-R BS.1770 [1], adopted by the European Broadcasting Union as R128 [2]. Its core idea is that a sound pressure level in dB SPL does not correspond to perceived loudness; the ear's non-flat frequency response must be modelled before measuring mean-square energy. BS.1770 therefore filters the signal with a two-stage K-weighting filter and computes a gated mean square:

LK = −0.691 + 10 log10 (Σi Gi · zi) ,  zi = (1/T) ∫ yK2(t) dt

where Gi are channel weights (1.0 for mono) and yK is the K-weighted signal. R128 defines three integration windows: Momentary (400 ms), Short-term (3 s) and Integrated (whole programme with two-stage gating at −70 LUFS absolute and −10 LU relative thresholds). The reference level is −23 LUFS.

Critical observation (design argument #1). R128 as specified is a measurement standard for broadcast programme delivery, not a control law for a live microphone. Its windows (400 ms / 3 s / infinite) are far too slow for conversational gain riding, and −23 LUFS is far too quiet for a communication channel where intelligibility and presence matter. Blindly closing the loop around Momentary loudness would produce audible pumping and late gain rides; the Integrated gated estimator is unusable online. The conclusion adopted in this project: reuse the K-weighting perception model of BS.1770, but not its time integration or target — use fast per-frame measurement with a comms-oriented target of −19 dBFS K-weighted RMS and telephony-style AGC ballistics.

2.2 K-weighting at 16 kHz — re-deriving instead of porting

BS.1770 publishes its biquad coefficients only for 48 kHz. A naïve port to SeaTime's 16 kHz would be wrong: bilinear-transformed coefficients are strictly rate-dependent. However, the published coefficients are merely the digital images of an analog prototype, and the analog parameters are also given (or recoverable) [3]:

StagePrototypeAnalog parametersRole
1 — Pre-filterHigh shelff0 = 1681.974 Hz, G = +3.985 dB, Q = 0.7071 (S = 1/√2)Models the +4 dB acoustic-ear resonance above ~1.7 kHz
2 — RLB filterHigh-passf0 = 38.13 Hz (41.9 Hz in some revisions), Q = 0.5Removes sub-audible / DC energy (weighting curve returns to 0 dB)

Because the prototypes are known, the correct procedure at any sampling rate is to re-apply the RBJ audio-EQ cookbook bilinear transforms [4] to the analog specifications at the actual processor.frequency, rather than copying constants. The implementation computes the coefficients at runtime in the first frame:

A = 10G/40;   ω0 = 2π f0/fs;   αshelf = sin ω0/2 · √[(A + 1/A)(1/S − 1) + 2]

and the shelf/high-pass difference equations are the standard RBJ forms normalised by a0. At 16 kHz the shelf coefficient is comfortably far from the Nyquist frequency (1682 Hz ≪ 8000 Hz), so bilinear warping is small and accuracy is within a fraction of a dB of the 48 kHz reference design. The RLB stage was instantiated at 60 Hz Q = 0.5 (the conservative published variant), which is inaudible for speech but effectively kills DC and rumble — an intentional deviation documented in §5.2.

2.3 Automatic Gain Control as a servo loop

An AGC is a feedback (or feed-forward) control loop that manipulates a variable gain so that a measured level tracks a reference. Writing the measured level in dB as ℓ(t) and the target as ℓT, the classical form is:

gdB(t+1) = gdB(t) + μ · [ ℓT − (ℓ(t) + gdB(t)) ]

with step size μ derived from time constants: μ = 1 − e−1/(τfs). Two properties matter in practice:

  • Asymmetric ballistics. Gain must fall quickly when someone shouts (attack, here 80 ms) but rise slowly when the talker stops (release, here 1.5 s), otherwise the loop amplifies silence between words — the classic "pumping" artefact.
  • Freezing. Updating the loop while the input is not speech guarantees drift: the loop will always drive whatever is present (noise, hum) toward the target. Coupling the update to a VAD flag converts the AGC from a leveler of everything into a leveler of speech only — the key decision that makes a single global target viable.
